Growing up multilingual

Raising children in two or more languages is common and safe, but some of the most famous “brain benefits” have shrunk under scrutiny, and the real costs are about time and effort, not harm. Here are the numbers behind the pros and cons.

Chapter 2

The pros that hold up

Start with the myth that worries parents most: bilingualism does not cause language delay. Bilingual babies babble, say first words and reach the 50-word mark on the same timetable as monolingual babies. Mixing languages in one sentence (code-switching) is normal, not a sign of confusion.

Pro · moderate evidence

Better at understanding other people’s thoughts

Theory of mind is knowing that other people have their own thoughts, which can differ from yours: a friend will look for a toy where they left it, even if you saw someone move it. Bilingual children tend to pass these tests somewhat earlier, perhaps because they constantly track who speaks which language. Across the pooled studies of — children the effect was small (d = —), rising to medium (d = —) once vocabulary differences were accounted for.

Schroeder (2018), meta-analysis. No sign of publication bias, but fewer studies than other topics.

Pro · strong evidence

Learning in two languages didn’t hurt, it helped reading

In Portland, kids were assigned to dual-language immersion by lottery, as close to a fair experiment as schools get. Immersion students were ahead in English reading, with no harm in maths or science.

How to read this: immersion kids compared with kids who applied but didn’t win a place. “+7 months” means they read English about as well as the other group would be expected to seven months further into the school year. Results translated from test scores into school time.

Steele et al. (2017), RAND. More than — lottery applicants in Portland, Oregon. One city, one well-designed study.

Pro · contested

A possible buffer in old age

In studies that look back at patients, bilinguals show dementia symptoms years later. Studies that follow people forward in time don’t find a lower risk, so think of this as “maybe delays symptoms”, not “prevents dementia”.

Brini et al. (2020), meta-analysis. Dots show the estimate; lines show the 95% confidence interval.

Pro · self-evident

The obvious one: more people to talk to

The best-supported benefit is the plainest. Researchers who doubt the brain-training claims still stress that the value of bilingualism lies in the languages themselves, family ties across generations, friendships, travel and careers. In the Eurobarometer, Europeans most often named job opportunities as the main reason to learn a language.

Chapter 3

The overhyped claim: “bilingual brains are sharper”

For years, headlines said that juggling two languages trains the brain’s control centre, which psychologists call executive function: paying attention, ignoring distractions and switching between tasks. If that were true, bilingual kids should score higher on those tests. The large modern reviews find almost no difference once you correct for publication bias, the tendency for exciting results to get published while “no difference” results stay in drawers. The biggest review pooled — bilingual and — monolingual children; a review of adults pooled — studies and found no advantage left after correction.

How big is the difference? “Sharper brains” vs. understanding others

Researchers measure how big a difference is with an effect size. Zero means bilingual and monolingual children score the same. Around 0.2 is a small difference you only see in averages across many children; around 0.5 is a medium one a careful teacher might notice.

Executive functionAttention, self-control and switching between tasks. This is the “sharper brain” claim.
Theory of mindUnderstanding that other people have their own thoughts and beliefs.

Each dot is one review’s pooled estimate: the combined result of many studies. The further a dot sits to the right of 0, the bigger the gap between bilingual and monolingual children. Point at a dot for details. Lowe et al. (2021), Psychological Science; Gunnerud et al. (2020), Psychological Bulletin; Schroeder (2018), Frontiers in Communication.

What this means: bilingualism probably won’t make your child “smarter” in general, so choose it for the languages. It may help children understand other people a little earlier, and the evidence shows no cognitive cost.

The real costs: time, vocabulary and the home language

What helps, for international-school families

  • Protect the home language’s “dose”Exposure matters more than any one method. Books, calls with grandparents, weekend classes, holidays and screen time in that language all add up.
  • Give the minority language more than half a chanceChildren tend to need more input in a minority language than in the dominant one to reach the same level, because school and friends tip the balance.
  • Read and write it, not only speak itLiteracy in the first language makes later academic learning in the second easier, and keeps the home language useful as children grow.
  • Expect a lag after a moveFluent playground chat doesn’t mean a child is ready for academic work in a new language. Ask the school about language support for several years, not months.
  • Don’t panic about mixingCode-switching is normal. If you’re worried about a delay, have a specialist assess both languages, a real disorder shows up in both.
  • Count words across languagesWhen checking development, a bilingual child’s combined vocabulary is the fair comparison with monolingual norms.

About the sources and how to read them

Figures come from peer-reviewed meta-analyses (which pool many studies), one large randomised lottery study, large surveys and widely cited classic studies. Effect sizes (d or g) express differences in standard deviations; by convention about a fifth of one is “small” and half of one is “medium”. Studies differ in country, languages and family background, so treat each number as a general pattern, not a prediction for any one child.
